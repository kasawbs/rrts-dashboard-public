About the Russian River Telemetry Study

The Russian River Telemetry Study is a research initiative led by the California State Water Resources Control Board to evaluate the use of telemetered monitoring equipment for measuring surface water and groundwater conditions in near real time.

The study focuses on installing, upgrading, and evaluating telemetry at monitoring locations throughout the Russian River watershed, including streamflow, reservoir, diversion, and groundwater sites. Data collected through this effort support research into modern water monitoring practices and help inform future telemetry implementation across California.

This dashboard provides a high-level view of study stations and available telemetry data. The information presented is intended for research and informational purposes only and does not represent regulatory determinations or compliance evaluations. Data may be incomplete, subject to change, or under review.

Participation in the Russian River Telemetry Study is voluntary, and data visibility may vary by station based on study status and data sharing agreements.

Learn more at the Russian River Telemetry website or watch this video.

Understanding the data sources

What each of the six data sources on this dashboard measures
i

Stations on this dashboard come from six different data sources. The Aquarius stations are the study's own telemetered monitoring sites. The other five sources are established reference networks and data exchanges operated by other agencies, shown alongside the study's data to give researchers context for comparison.

Aquarius

The Russian River Telemetry Study's own stations. Telemetered equipment installed or upgraded by the study measures streamflow, reservoir, diversion, and groundwater conditions in near real time at volunteer sites throughout the watershed. This is the core dataset the study is evaluating. Learn more about the Aquarius software.

USGS

The United States Geological Survey operates a nationwide network of long established streamflow and groundwater gages. USGS data provides a reliable, long term reference that helps researchers compare newly telemetered Aquarius readings against established federal measurements nearby. View the USGS National Water Dashboard.

CDEC

The California Data Exchange Center, operated by the California Department of Water Resources, collects real time streamflow, reservoir, and precipitation data from stations across the state, including several in the Russian River watershed. CDEC fills in regional coverage alongside the study's own sites. Visit the California Data Exchange Center.

SonomaOne

Sonoma Water, formerly the Sonoma County Water Agency, maintains its own telemetered gages on streams and reservoirs in the county, largely for flood forecasting and water supply management. Including this data adds a local, agency operated perspective that complements the study's research-focused stations. View Sonoma Water flood and water level information.

CNRA

The California Natural Resources Agency publishes periodic groundwater level measurements collected under the Sustainable Groundwater Management Act. Unlike the continuously telemetered sources above, CNRA readings are collected on a periodic schedule. Browse the CNRA Open Data portal.

WQX

The Water Quality Exchange is a shared data format and portal, used by the EPA, USGS, and state and local agencies nationwide, for publishing discrete water quality sample results. WQX stations shown on this dashboard are separate from the continuous streamflow and level telemetry collected from the other public reference sources. Visit the Water Quality Portal.

About the telemetry technology

Why the study is testing telemetered monitoring equipment
i

Telemetry means equipment at a monitoring site automatically measures conditions, such as streamflow, water level, or diversion rate, and transmits those readings to a central system, such as by cellular or satellite. Readings and transmissions can be as frequent as the project requires. For this project, we are testing 5-minute to 15-minute intervals.

Traditional monitoring requires a person to visit a monitoring site, read a gage or download a datalogger file, save the documentation, and transfer the data into the central system.

Note: This section describes the research goals of the Telemetry Pilot Project. It does not represent a regulatory position or a finding about any individual participant reporting obligations.

About Station Maintenance

i

This page helps staff spot stations that may be worth a site visit or an equipment check. It looks for two kinds of conditions: a streamflow or water-level reading sitting near the very top or very bottom of that station's own historic range (which can mean the reading is near the edge of what the rating curve or sensor can reliably measure), and a diversion or underflow well that has been flowing without a break for a long stretch of time (which can point to a stuck valve, a sensor that isn't updating, or simply ongoing use worth confirming).

This is not a flood warning. It is a separate, internal maintenance signal. For public flood-risk information, see the Flood Watch page.

About Flood Watch

i

This page highlights stations whose most recent streamflow or water-level reading stands out compared to what's typically been seen at that location, so you can quickly spot stations that may be worth a closer look. For most stations, the "Condition" column compares the current reading to that station's highest reading in this dataset (this dataset starts in ). For a few gage-height stations that are also published National Weather Service (NWS) Advanced Hydrologic Prediction Service (AHPS) flood gauges, marked Official NWS stage in the table, the condition instead compares the current reading to that gauge's real published NWS action and flood stages.

Telemetered stations report far more often than manual readings, so a rising river or stream can be noticed within minutes or hours instead of days, giving operators, emergency managers, and the public more time to make timely flood-risk decisions. See the Help & Resources page for more detail.

This is not an official flood warning. For official flood forecasts and warnings at every location, see the National Weather Service Advanced Hydrologic Prediction Service or CDEC.

What the stages and groupings mean

A guide to reading these charts
i

Water data from stations are processed in five successive stages. Each stage adds more data cleaning, so the same reading can appear several times at different levels of review.

  • S1 - raw: the sensor reading exactly as it arrived, with no conversion or review. Expect gaps, spikes and dropouts here. This is the right stage for checking whether telemetry is getting through.
  • S2 - converted: the same data converted into feet in the local datum, so the numbers are comparable between sites.
  • S3 - automatic checks: automated quality control screening has flagged or removed obviously bad values.
  • S4 - reviewed: a hydrologist has manually screened the record and applied corrections informed by field visits.
  • S5 - final: the published record for the water year. It may be assembled from the best parts of several S4 series.

The other groupings describe where each reading came from:

  • Sensor type: the instrument that took the measurement, such as a pressure transducer (PT), bubbler, radar, or water quality sensor (WQ). Where two of the same sensor are installed at one site they are labelled PT1 and PT2.
  • Telemetry type: how the reading was transmitted back, by cellular, satellite or LoRa, or collected on site as logged data.
  • Equipment type: which piece of equipment reported the value. At LoRa sites that monitor two batteries, these appear as pico and digi.
  • Default: readings with no sensor, telemetry, or equipment metadata are grouped under this label.

Two notes that explain gaps you may see. Where one sensor sends data over several telemetry paths, only one of those series is carried through to S4, because manually reviewing all of them would be duplicated effort; compare those at S1 instead. And parameters outside the study goals, such as battery voltage, are only ever processed to S1.

Understanding station status

What the Network Status card and station status tags mean
i

The Network status of stations reporting data card on the Station Overview page breaks down all station records by reporting recency and data access policy level. Each station's status reflects the volunteer's data-sharing agreement for that site. It does not depend on whether the station's location is shown precisely, generalized to a watershed area, or withheld. Location visibility is shown separately on the station detail page and map.

  • Reporting (24 h) : The station has reported a reading within the last 24 hours. Parameter readings and CSV download are both available.
  • Last 30 days : The station has reported a reading within the last 30 days but not within the last 24 hours.
  • Dormant (>30 d) : The station has not reported a reading in over 30 days. Data may still be available for download.
  • Limited by policy : Either the parameter readings or the CSV download is withheld by the volunteer's data-sharing agreement, but not both. The station may still show some data on the dashboard.
  • Restricted by policy : Both parameter readings and CSV download are withheld by the volunteer's data-sharing agreement for this station. No data is visible on the dashboard and no download is available.
  • Setup pending : The station is configured in the study roster but has not yet ingested any data. This typically means the station is not yet set up or is awaiting initial data collection.
  • No data : No readings have been received yet, or recency could not be determined (non-Aquarius stations with no data).

Note: The "Aquarius study stations overview" card in Explore Study Stations uses a different classification (Live reporting, Data not published, and Setup pending) which reflects how many readings are actually visible to dashboard users, not the policy level. A station can be "Restricted by policy" in the Network Status card yet still show some published readings in the Aquarius study stations overview card (e.g., in an internal build), or vice versa.

Aquarius station publication status

What "Live reporting", "Data not published", and "Setup pending" mean for Aquarius stations
i

The Aquarius study stations overview card in Explore Study Stations classifies each Aquarius station by how much of its data is actually visible to dashboard users. This is different from the Network Status card, which reflects the policy level.

  • Live reporting : All of the station's readings are published and visible on the dashboard. The station is actively reporting data and the volunteer's data-sharing agreement allows full publication.
  • Partially published : Some of the station's readings are visible on the dashboard while others are withheld by the volunteer's data-sharing agreement or by policy. The station is reporting, but not all data is accessible.
  • Data not published : None of the station's readings are visible on the dashboard, and the station has two or more readings withheld by the volunteer's data-sharing agreement or by policy. The station has data in the system but it is fully restricted.
  • Setup pending : The station is configured in the study roster but has not yet ingested meaningful data (zero or one reading withheld). This means the station is not yet set up, is awaiting initial data collection, or the telemetry equipment has not yet been installed and commissioned.

Note: A station's publication status in this card may differ from its status in the Network Status card. For example, a station may be "Restricted by policy" in the Network Status card (both parameters and download withheld) yet still show "Live reporting" here if some readings are visible in an internal build. Conversely, a station may show "Data not published" here while being "Limited by policy" in the Network Status card.

Understanding location status

What the location status shown on the Station Detail page and map means
i

The Location Status shown on the Station Detail page and in the station list on the Station Map page reflects the volunteer's data-sharing agreement for the station's geographic coordinates. It is independent of the station's data reporting status. Restricted locations are masked using Constrained Geomasking: a random point is selected within the HUC watershed boundary, so the displayed point is always inside the correct watershed but does not reveal the exact station position.

  • Precise : Exact latitude and longitude coordinates are shown on the map and in the station detail. The station appears as a small dot.
  • In HUC12 : A random point is selected within the HUC12 watershed boundary. The station appears as a small semi-transparent bubble on the map.
  • In HUC10 : A random point is selected within the HUC10 watershed boundary. The station appears as a medium semi-transparent bubble on the map.
  • In HUC8 : A random point is selected within the HUC8 watershed boundary. The station appears as a large semi-transparent bubble on the map.
  • Hidden : Coordinates are fully withheld by the volunteer's data-sharing agreement. The station does not appear on the map and its location is not shown in the station detail.

Note: Location status does not affect whether data is visible or downloadable. A station can have "Hidden" location status while still reporting data and allowing downloads.

Why compare multiple Aquarius processing stages?

The reasoning behind the five processing stages shown on the Explore Study Stations page
i

The Explore Study Stations page lets you compare a station's reading across five Aquarius processing stages, from the rawest logged sensor output through to the final, reviewed record. There are two main reasons for showing all of them instead of just the final value.

First, comparing stages is a quality assurance check. It lets staff track how a reading changes at each step of data transformation, so unexpected or unwanted changes introduced during processing can be caught rather than assumed away.

Second, reporters submit data at different levels of formatting: some provide the rawest logged values, while others provide data that has already been cleaned and structured before it ever reaches this system. Comparing stages side by side helps determine whether there is any real, meaningful difference between those levels, and whether waiting for the final, late-reviewed stage actually adds real value compared to using an earlier, faster-available stage.

Trade-off: publishing every stage multiplies the amount of data significantly. That cost is worth showing because it illustrates a broader challenge for the study: balancing the quality and quantity of data that is actually needed to make the best water-management decisions.

Why do stations use different sensors and telemetry?

Why equipment and transmission methods vary from station to station
i

Stations in the study do not all use the same equipment. Some have a single sensor and telemetry type, while others combine more than one. This is a deliberate choice, not an inconsistency, because every site is different.

What a station needs to measure, how much power and cellular or satellite signal is available at that location, and how well equipment holds up in cold or harsh conditions, all shape which sensors and transmission methods make sense there. A station optimized for one of these constraints may need a different setup than a station nearby that faces different conditions.

In short, the variety of sensors and telemetry types across the study reflects the genuine diversity of California's watersheds, climates, and site conditions, not a lack of standardization.

Importance of Flood Watch

How real-time telemetered data supports timely flood-risk decisions
i

Streamflow and water-level conditions can change quickly during storms, so how often a station is checked matters. Traditional manual readings may only happen periodically, which can miss the window when conditions are changing fastest. Telemetered stations report automatically and far more frequently, often multiple times per hour, so rising conditions can be identified in near real time.

That earlier notice gives water managers, emergency responders, and the public more time to act: adjusting operations, issuing warnings, planning evacuations, or simply deciding whether to keep a closer eye on a location, before conditions become critical. The Flood Watch page is built on that same real-time data, so it can only highlight a changing condition as quickly as the underlying station is reporting it.

This is why continued investment in telemetered monitoring, rather than relying solely on periodic manual readings, directly supports faster, better-informed flood risk management across the watershed.

How is flood risk calculated?

What drives the condition shown on the Flood Watch page
i

The Flood Watch page only looks at two kinds of readings: streamflow and gage height (water surface elevation, also called stage). These are the two measurements that drive flood-risk pages on NWS, CDEC, and USGS sites, and they are the only ones RRTS stations report often enough to support this feature.

A station and parameter only shows up on the page once it has at least 30 historical readings in this dataset. Below that, there is not enough history to meaningfully say a reading is "near a record," so the station is left out of Flood Watch entirely until it has enough data.

Two different methods decide the condition, depending on the station:

  • Official NWS stage. A small number of gage-height stations are also published National Weather Service flood-forecast gauges with real, publicly issued thresholds: an action stage and a minor flood stage, in feet. For these stations, the current reading is compared directly to those published thresholds. At or above minor flood stage (the NWS's own definition of flood stage) is shown as Near record high, at or above action stage but below minor flood stage is Elevated, and below action stage is Normal. A few gauges only publish a flood stage with no separate action stage; for those, 75% of the published flood stage is used as the elevated watch point instead, so the Elevated tier is not skipped.
  • Dataset-relative. For every other station, including all streamflow readings, there is no published official threshold to compare against. Instead, the station's current reading is compared to the single highest reading for that station and parameter in the RRTS dataset (this dataset starts in ) ("percent of record"). 90% or more of that historic max is Near record high, 75 to 90% is Elevated, and below 75% is Normal. This is a relative, dataset-specific comparison, not an officially recognized flood threshold, which is why the table labels it "Dataset-relative" rather than citing a flood stage.

Important: the condition shown is based only on the single most recent reading at the time of the last dashboard build. It is not a sustained-duration measure: there is no requirement that a reading stay elevated for any length of time, and no trend or rate-of-rise is factored in. A single unusually high or noisy reading can move a station into a higher tier, and the next reading can move it back down. Treat Flood Watch as a quick, build-time snapshot of current conditions relative to history, not as an official flood forecast or warning.

CalWATRS Reporting Format

Why the Download tab offers a CalWATRS-formatted export
i

Starting Water Year 2027 (October 2026), the State Water Board's updated Water Measurement Regulation requires diverters of more than 10 acre-feet per year to submit standardized measurement data to CalWATRS (California Water Accounting, Tracking, and Reporting System). The CalWATRS format option on the Download tab builds a file shaped to that standard, with Date, Time, Measured Value, Volume Diverted, and Flow Rate of Diversion columns, each with its units, so it can be used directly for reporting. See the 2026 Measurement and Reporting Manual for full requirements.

How Volume Diverted is calculated: telemetry sensors record instantaneous flow rate (cubic feet per second), not volume. When a selected station has flow data, Volume Diverted is calculated, not directly measured, by integrating that day's flow readings over time and converting to acre-feet. Flow Rate of Diversion is reported as the measured flow rate itself. Stations without a flow parameter have no diversion data to report, so they are excluded from the CalWATRS export, and a warning is shown on the Download tab.

Note: only the parameters required for CalWATRS reporting (flow and the volume calculated from it) are included in that export, even if other parameters are selected. Other readings, such as water quality, do not need to be reported in CalWATRS, so they are left out of that file.
